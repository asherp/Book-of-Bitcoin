The queue above is ordered as a miner would take it, which begs the question of who the miners are. The chain never says. A chapter carries a signature in its opening line — /Foundry USA Pool/, Mined by AntPool — and the book quotes that writing to its own extent; but a tag is unauthenticated, and no field anywhere states a pool's share of the hashrate. Every "pool X has Y% of the network" figure is inferred the same way: count the chapters each signature won over some trailing window, and divide. Winning blocks is the only census there is.

And winning blocks is a lottery. Each chapter is one draw, won by a pool holding a share p of the hashing with probability p — so a count over N blocks reads back p give or take √(p(1−p)/N), the binomial's own sampling error, before anything about the network has changed at all. The windows dashboards quote are short. Over a day — 144 blocks — a pool truly holding 30% is measured to ±3.8 points, landing outside 26–34 one day in three. Most "pool nears 51%" headlines are this arithmetic and nothing else: a one- or two-sigma day, reported as an event.

Widening the window buys precision and spends currency. A count over months is exact about a distribution that may no longer exist, because hashrate moves: when GHash.io brushed half the network in the summer of 2014, the exodus that followed took weeks, and any season-long window went on reporting a crowd that had already left. Every window is a bet that the distribution held still while it was being counted. For a snapshot of the present, a book or two of the chain — 2,016 to 4,032 blocks, the difficulty window this book binds its chapters by, two to four weeks — is about where the two errors trade evenly. The smallest pool worth resolving sets the floor: holding a share p to within a quarter of itself, at two sigma, takes about 64·(1−p)/p blocks — a hundred and fifty for a 30% pool, six thousand and more for a 1% one.

A hard window carries one artifact of its own: a lucky morning falls out of it whole, exactly 144 blocks later, and the estimate steps on a day nothing happened. The bookkeeping that avoids this is the exponentially weighted average — after every block, `p̂ ← λ·p̂ + (1−λ)·won`, one accumulator per pool, `won` being 1 for the winner and 0 for everyone else, so the shares always sum to one. Every block ever mined is still in the figure, at a weight that fades geometrically, and nothing ever falls off an edge. The memory is set by λ: the effective sample size is (1+λ)/(1−λ), so λ = 0.999 weighs like the 2,016-block window above, with a half-life near 700 blocks — about five days. A fresh accumulator undercounts everyone until it has seen enough; divide by 1−λᵗ, or let it run a few half-lives before quoting it.

Smoothing buys its stability with lag: after a genuine step — a new pool switched on, an exodus under way — the estimate covers half the distance in one half-life, most of a week. So the honest instrument is two gauges, not one. Beside the smoothed share, check the raw last day against what that share promises: 144·p̂ wins expected, with σ = √(144·p̂(1−p̂)), and z the day's shortfall or excess in units of σ. A z past three that holds for a run of days flags a real shift within a day of it — long before the smoothed figure has finished catching up — while a z that spikes once and reverts is the lottery being a lottery.

All of which counts pools, and pools are not miners: the hashing is done by machines whose owners can point them elsewhere between one block and the next, which is how GHash.io's share came down — by defection, not by any machine switching off. The share is also the honest form of the figure: a ratio of blocks won cancels the noise in estimating the network's whole hashrate, which an absolute EH/s number inherits on top of everything above. And it remains, from the first count to the last, a reading rather than a record — a tag is writing anyone may copy, and a percentage is an inference from it. The book's rule for readings holds here with unusual force: publish the uncertainty beside the point estimate and name the window it was counted over, or what is being published is not a measurement but a headline.
