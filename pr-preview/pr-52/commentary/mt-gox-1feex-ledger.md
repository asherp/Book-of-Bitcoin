A name known by its first five characters, and a record with one direction: 690 outputs received, 79,957.26866953 ₿ in all, none ever spent. The first arrival is the whole story — 79,956 ₿ exactly, 1 March 2011, the sweep of Mt. Gox's addresses kept in the contents as Mt. Gox robbed of 79,956 ₿ (I β56 ■315 §2). The other 689 outputs are the fifteen years since: 1.26866953 ₿ of small sends from the world to a famous address — tributes, tests, dust, and reportedly phishing bait — none of which can be told apart on the chain from any other arrival.

Two claims have been made on this name in public, and a ledger is where the book sets such claims apart from the record they read. The estate's: Mark Karpelès stated in August 2023 that the coins are stolen property of the Mt. Gox estate, held for its creditors — the operator's own accounting of the theft, down to the 79,956.55 ₿ figure that matches the sweep's inputs to the satoshi. And the claim the courts ended: Tulip Trading Ltd, Craig Wright's vehicle, sued sixteen bitcoin developers in 2021 to be handed control of this address, asserting its keys had been Wright's and were lost in a hack — an action that collapsed with its claimant when the COPA judgment of 2024 found Wright had lied and forged documents on a grand scale, and was discontinued in its wake.

The record supports neither claim, nor any other. An address is not an owner, and an unspent balance is patience, not identity: whether these keys are held carefully, held by no one, or lost is written nowhere the chain can show. What the ledger states on its own authority is narrower and harder: for fifteen years, against every incentive, under two claims and a bankruptcy, not one satoshi of this name has ever moved.
