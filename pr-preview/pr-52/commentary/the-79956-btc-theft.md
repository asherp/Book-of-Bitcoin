Twenty-seven inputs, worth 79,956.55 ₿ together, are gathered and paid — 79,956 ₿ exactly — to a single address, with 0.55 ₿ of change and no fee. 1 March 2011. Nothing in the passage is irregular: the signatures are valid, the scripts are standard, and the chain accepted it as it accepts any spend, because the chain checks that the keyholder signed, never that the keyholder was the owner.

The claim that makes this section a theft — the largest of its era, and the reason this page is in the contents — is the operator's own. Mark Karpelès, who bought Mt. Gox from Jed McCaleb the same week these coins moved, stated publicly in August 2023 that 79,956.55 ₿ left the exchange on this date without authorization, stolen property of the Mt. Gox estate: his figure matches this section's input total to the satoshi. The mechanism usually given is a copied wallet.dat file, which fits the shape of the page — many funded addresses swept into one output — but is reporting, not record. No one has ever been charged; the 2023 U.S. indictment over Mt. Gox reaches back only to September 2011, and this page precedes it by half a year.

What the chain adds is the ending no report could write: the coins have never moved. The receiving address, known by its first characters as 1Feex, has spent nothing in fifteen years — its whole record reads in the Ledger as The 1Feex hoard. A theft is invisible in the passage it leaves, because a stolen coin moves by a valid signature; what this page shows is only a withdrawal, perfectly formed. The theft is a reading beside it — the operator's, the estate's, this book's — and the unspent balance is the one part of the story the record tells by itself.
