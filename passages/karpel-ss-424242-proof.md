## Commentary

> A reading of the record, not the record. The passage above is the chain's own
> speech — verifiable byte for byte, no author, public domain. What follows is

Nine inputs totaling 442,000 ₿ even; one output of 424,242.42424242 ₿; change of 17,757.57575758 ₿ — subtract the joke number from the round total and a second repeating decimal is forced — and no fee. 23 June 2011.

The section is an exchange speaking. Three days earlier an attacker had used a compromised administrator account to credit themselves coins in Mt. Gox's database and sell them into the order book, printing bitcoin at $0.01 and closing the exchange; the balances were rolled back, roughly 2,000 ₿ were reported actually withdrawn, and none of it — the fake coins, the crash, the rollback — touched the chain at all. With solvency in open doubt, Mark Karpelès offered to move a uniquely identifiable amount as proof the exchange still held its coins, and chose 424,242.42424242. A signature is the only sentence the chain can verify, so the answer to the doubt was this page.

The proof was real and it was bounded, and both halves are the lesson. Control of 442,000 ₿ at this block is exactly what the section proves — not solvency, and nothing about coins already gone or keys yet to be copied: the 79,956 ₿ taken that March (Mt. Gox robbed of 79,956 ₿ — I β56 ■315 §2, sixteen weeks earlier in this same volume) were already sitting unspent where the thief put them, and that September the wallet keys would be copied and the exchange drained over two years by thousands of transactions shaped exactly like withdrawals. The proof coins moved on that July and dispersed. With hindsight the page is the era's high-water mark: the last day anyone could sign for Mt. Gox's coins and be believed.
