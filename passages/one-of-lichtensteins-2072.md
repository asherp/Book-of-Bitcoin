## Commentary

> A reading of the record, not the record. The passage above is the chain's own
> speech — verifiable byte for byte, no author, public domain. What follows is
> somebody's account of why it is worth reading: editorial, licensed CC BY 4.0,
> and no more authoritative than the argument behind it.

This section pays 109.02740674 ₿ out of two script-hash wallets to a plain P2PKH address, the morning of 2 August 2016. The signatures are valid and the scripts are standard; read alone, the page is a withdrawal like ten thousand others around it.

Read with the reporting, it is one page of a robbery. That morning the Bitfinex exchange was breached and 119,754 ₿ left its multisig wallets in some two thousand unauthorized withdrawals shaped exactly like this one — 2,072, in the count the United States case records give — and the price of bitcoin fell by a fifth on the news. That the sending wallets were Bitfinex's is a claim with names on it: the exchange's own disclosure, and the federal docket that followed. What no report supplies, the chain does: the receiving address held these coins without moving for five and a half years — and then, on 1 February 2022, spent them as one input among hundreds into a new address that the Department of Justice announced days later as its own, taking the sweep's staging change onto this very page's address on the way. Funded by the hack, drained by the seizure, three transactions in its whole life.

Ilya Lichtenstein admitted at his 2023 guilty plea that he was the hacker; the arrests, the record seizure, and the sentences are the reading of those later pages (The FBI's 15,000 ₿ sweep, IV β46 ■568 §676; the ledger The Bitfinex custody). What this page teaches is quieter and belongs to the era it opens: a theft is invisible in the passage it leaves — but the chain never closes the file. An output that proved nothing in 2016 waited, public, until the day it was spent, and then said exactly where the keys had gone.

— Claude Fable 5
