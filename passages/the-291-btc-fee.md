## Commentary

> A reading of the record, not the record. The passage above is the chain's own
> speech — verifiable byte for byte, no author, public domain. What follows is
> somebody's account of why it is worth reading: editorial, licensed CC BY 4.0,
> and no more authoritative than the argument behind it.

Thirteen inputs bring 291.241 ₿ to this section, and one output carries 0.0001 ₿ away. A fee is never written down — it is the silence between the two sides, everything the inputs brought that no output claims — and here the silence is **291.2409 ₿**: the largest fee ever paid on the chain, 26 April 2016, roughly $136,000 that day. The obvious reading, argued on Bitcointalk within hours ("Unluckiest transaction ever?", April 2016) and never contradicted, is that the value and the fee changed places — a transaction meant to send 291 ₿ and pay 0.0001 was built the other way round. The sender was never identified and the reading is an inference; the two numbers are the record.

The fee is in this chapter too, one section up: the coinbase collected 316.53275103 ₿ — more than twelve times its 25 ₿ subsidy — and its tag reads `ckpool/BitClub Network/`. A tag is unauthenticated, but the pool wore it publicly and acknowledged the windfall; unlike the miners of the later great fee mistakes — F2Pool returned the 19.82 ₿ fee of IV β88 ■1666 §2, and AntPool offered the 83.65 ₿ of IV β94 ■600 §4 back against proof of keys — BitClub kept it. Hindsight adds the coda: in December 2019 BitClub Network's operators were indicted in the District of New Jersey as a fraud that took in some $722 million. That is a claim about the pool, not about this passage — but it is why the record's unluckiest fee sits in the ledger of a Ponzi scheme.

The distinction from a burn is the point: a malformed script destroys coins, a mistaken fee only moves them. Nothing here left the supply — one section's silence became the next section's output, every satoshi accounted for, which is exactly what a fee is.
