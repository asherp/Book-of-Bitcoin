## Commentary

> A reading of the record, not the record. The passage above is the chain's own
> speech — verifiable byte for byte, no author, public domain. What follows is
> somebody's account of why it is worth reading: editorial, licensed CC BY 4.0,

Thirty-three inputs; one output of 40,000.32735811 ₿. 25 March 2022. The section is the heart of a five-transaction sweep — a 0.1 ₿ test, an 8,999.90803426, this 40,000, and two remnants — that moved 49,825.72380688 ₿ into one new address inside an hour.

The reading is a decade wide. In September 2012, James Zhong opened about nine accounts on the Silk Road market and triggered more than 140 withdrawals in rapid succession — five withdrawals of a 500 ₿ deposit inside five seconds, in the indictment's example — tricking the market's payout system into releasing about 50,000 ₿ he had never deposited. He consolidated the take into two amounts, roughly 40,000 and 10,000, and largely let them sit. On 9 November 2021, agents raided his house in Georgia and took the keys from an underground floor safe and from a computer buried in a popcorn tin in a bathroom closet; this page is those keys spending, four months later, into government custody. The theft's shape had survived every intervening shuffle: the larger of the two 2012 hoards arrives here as a single undivided output, the indictment's "two high-value amounts" still legible in the seizure's own arithmetic.

Zhong pleaded guilty to wire fraud that November and was sentenced to a year and a day; the government moved the 40,000 once more — still whole — in March 2023, and began selling. Set beside the Mt. Gox record earlier in the book (Mt. Gox robbed of 79,956 ₿, I β56 ■315 §2), the entry closes a register the book keeps returning to: Mt. Gox's thieves were charged but never caught and the coins never found; Zhong was caught because the coins were found, held too still for too long by keys that could be seized with a house. On every page along the way — fraud, decade, seizure, sale — the chain wrote only valid spends. Who a coin belonged to was never on any of them; only who could sign.
