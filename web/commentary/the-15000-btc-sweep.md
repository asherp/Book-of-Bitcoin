Five hundred and sixty-two inputs; one output; 15,000 ₿ even. 1 February 2022, before five in the morning UTC. The section is the largest single passage of an operation that runs across four chapters and 133 minutes: a 1 ₿ test send first, then sweep after sweep — 10,000 ₿ from 223 addresses, this 15,000 and a twin behind it, four more 10,000s in a later chapter (one drawing on 592 addresses), the odd lots, and finally a 510-input pass that gathers exactly 0.29837084 ₿ of dust. Twenty-three transactions, 94,643.29837084 ₿, one destination.

The reading arrived a week later with names attached: the Department of Justice announced it had seized the proceeds of the 2016 Bitfinex hack — the largest financial seizure in its history, some $3.6 billion at the day's price — and arrested Ilya Lichtenstein and Heather Morgan. Agents had decrypted a cloud-stored file holding the private keys to two thousand hack addresses, and these pages are those keys at work: the state spending the thief's coins with the thief's own signatures. Which is the entry's point. A seizure and a theft are the same sentence on the chain — a valid spend by whoever holds the keys — and nothing in this section says which one it is. In 2016 the signer was a robber; in 2022, a marshal; the pages are indistinguishable, and only the readings beside them differ.

Lichtenstein admitted being the hacker at his 2023 plea; he drew five years, Morgan eighteen months. One of his withdrawals — and the address it paid, drained into these very sweeps — reads a volume earlier as One of Lichtenstein's 2,072 (III β2 ■1284 §719). The coins themselves have not moved: the custody address has spent nothing since, and its whole record — the sweeps, and four years of the world's dust arriving after them — reads in the Ledger as The Bitfinex custody.
