A name whose entire record was written in 133 minutes, and then never again in one direction: 23 transactions on the morning of 1 February 2022 delivered 94,643.29837084 ₿ — a 1 ₿ test, sweeps of ten and fifteen thousand drawing on hundreds of addresses apiece, and a final 510-input pass collecting exactly 0.29837084 ₿ of dust. Since that morning: 158 more arrivals totaling 0.18986935 ₿ — the world's small sends to a famous address — and not one satoshi ever out.

The name is the state's. A week after the sweeps the Department of Justice announced the largest financial seizure in its history: the proceeds of the August 2016 Bitfinex hack, in which 119,754 ₿ left the exchange in some two thousand unauthorized withdrawals. Agents had decrypted a file in Ilya Lichtenstein's cloud storage holding the keys to the hack's addresses, and the sweeps are those keys signing for their new keeper — one of the hack's own withdrawals, and the address it paid, reads in the contents as One of the 2,072, and the largest sweep as The 15,000 ₿ sweep. Lichtenstein admitted he was the hacker; the sentences came in 2024, and where the coins go next belongs to the forfeiture docket, not the chain.

The shelf keeps this ledger a row from the 1Feex hoard, and the pairing is the reading: the chain's two great one-way records of stolen coins, one held by a thief nobody has named, one held by the state that named him. Their tables are indistinguishable — arrivals, dust, patience — because custody is not a script property: lawful and unlawful holding print alike, and only the readings beside a name can say which is which. The record states what it can on its own authority: everything that arrived is still here.
